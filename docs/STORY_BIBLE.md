# ASHEN VOW

## The promise

A cinematic, isometric action game about people who survived by obeying—and what happens when they finally refuse. Each expedition is a small dramatic arc: arrival, discovery, confrontation, consequence. The first playable chapter follows a Japanese samurai into a courtyard where his former commander has spent seven years preventing a bell from ringing.

This is an invented world with distinct, recognisable cultural roots, not a claim of historical accuracy. Japanese, Chinese, Indian, and English characters have separate homes, institutions, languages, and martial traditions. Their common enemy does not make their cultures interchangeable. Cultural reference research and consultation belong before final production art and voice work.

## The world: a peace bought with names

The Quiet Accord ended a war without settling its causes. Its ministers discovered that a name, willingly surrendered at the moment of death, could hold a memory inside metal. Bells preserved the last words of the dead. Families visited them. Cities built shrines around them. For a generation, remembrance became a public service.

Then the Accord learned to take names without permission. A community stripped of its dead lost the memory of why it had resisted. Commanders became saints. Massacres became unfortunate weather. The bells were no longer memorials; they were instruments of government.

The ministers call this mercy. Their officials can remove the memory of a particular person while leaving behind the shape of grief: an empty place at dinner, a habit of turning toward a voice, a room nobody can explain. The world looks intact because most people cannot remember what is missing.

Four survivors discover different parts of this machinery. None begins as the chosen saviour. Each wants something smaller and more dangerous: a sister, a confession, an accurate record, the right to bury a stranger. Their stories eventually meet at the place where names are melted down.

## Ren Takamori — the samurai who returned late

Ren grew up counting. His father measured rice, his mother kept household accounts, and his younger sister Aki counted the intervals between temple bells. Lord Kuroda noticed Ren's patience during a levy inspection and took him into military service. Ren learned to wait through the first movement of an attack, to notice which soldier was limping, and to count everyone back through a gate.

That attention made him an exceptional samurai and a poor instrument of unquestioning command. During the last winter of the war, he received an order to seal a road against an approaching rebel column. Refugees arrived first. Kuroda promised that a second route remained open. Ren held his position. When smoke rose behind the hills, he stayed because abandoning the road would expose the men in his care.

The army called the operation a victory. Ren came home to find his family's house standing and his mother unable to explain the second bedding roll. He remembered Aki because she had scratched her name into the guard of his sword as a joke before he left. The metal retained what the bell had taken.

For seven years he has followed missing names through burnt registers and abandoned shrines. He is not emotionless: he is exacting, dryly funny when exhausted, and uncomfortable receiving kindness he cannot repay. He helps people in practical ways, sharpening a tool or fixing a latch, because reassurance feels like another promise he may fail to keep.

His first lead is Kuroda, guarding the bell above the road he once sealed. Ren wants to hear that his commander lied. The harder truth is that Kuroda did lie, and Ren still made a choice.

**Visual language:** soot-black lamellar plates, a rain-heavy indigo coat, a narrow vermilion waist cord, and a chipped ivory sword guard. His silhouette is compact and human; the loose coat reveals controlled footwork. No enormous ornament competes with the sword. His idle hand checks the scratched name when he thinks nobody is looking.

**Combat:** deliberate katana cuts, brief evasive steps, and a deflection that turns precise timing into a decisive opening. His future signature ability, Witness, preserves the trace of an enemy's last attack so he can cut across it. His mechanics reward attention rather than endless aggression.

**Arc:** he begins by trying to recover one person and ends by accepting responsibility for people he will never meet. His victory is not forgiveness from the dead. It is refusing another convenient order.

## Lady Eleanor Vale — the knight who signed the warrant

Eleanor is an English knight from a rain-worn border household, the second daughter trained to administer an estate that could not afford another war. Her reputation began with a siege she ended through negotiation. She kept the granary intact, paid the garrison's wages, and allowed defeated soldiers to return home. The crown called her its steady hand.

During the following famine, she signed an order transferring villagers to an Accord relief house. The document was immaculate. Its witnesses were respected. The relief house had no kitchen.

Years later she found a surviving child carrying a list of names Eleanor recognised from the warrant. The child did not ask for vengeance. She asked where her mother was buried. Eleanor knew the location of every official involved, every archive, every supply road—and had no answer.

She left court with the original warrant sewn inside her surcoat. She refuses to burn it or hide her seal. Her investigation is an attempt to establish evidence sufficient to condemn herself alongside those who designed the system. This puts her at odds with companions who need her alive and enemies who would happily make her the sole guilty party.

Eleanor is courteous, observant, and capable of devastating understatement. She tends to treat a problem as a task that can be completed correctly. Her hardest lesson is that confession does not settle a debt, and that the people she harmed have the right to refuse her help.

**Visual language:** late-medieval English plate references, a shortened weathered blue surcoat, brass repair staples, a small thorn emblem, and a visor she closes only when she has made a decision. The armour bears maintenance rather than ornamental damage. Her silhouette is broad, stable, and readable beside Ren's narrow line.

**Combat:** longsword binds, armoured advances, and a compact shield used to protect space. A future guard ability lets her intercept an attack aimed at an ally or civilian. Her power is commitment; a badly chosen advance cannot be cancelled instantly.

**Signature encounter:** The Ledger Chapel, a flooded court archive. Her former squire guards the original relief registers, believing their destruction will spare the victims' families. The fight is over who gets to decide which truths are survivable.

## Lin Yue — the swordswoman who corrected the record

Lin Yue was raised beside a canal in a family of printers and trained in jian swordsmanship by an aunt who taught her to distinguish grace from wasted movement. She became a travelling copyist for a provincial archive, recording floods, land disputes, births, and military losses. She loved the modest authority of an accurate sentence.

Her downfall began with a correction. A village listed as abandoned was still inhabited. Its residents remembered a harvest and a funeral but could not name the person buried. Lin added the discrepancy to the official register. Her supervisor removed it. She added it again, attaching witnesses.

The Accord seized the printing house. Her father surrendered his own name in exchange for her release. He survived, but no longer recognised the work of his hands or the daughter who carried him home. Lin now distributes forbidden accounts disguised as ordinary broadsheets: prices, weather, ferry schedules, and the names of those missing from public history.

She is sociable, impatient with mystification, and suspicious of anyone who insists that suffering is too profound to describe plainly. Her flaw is believing a true account automatically leads to a just response. People can read a fact and choose comfort anyway.

**Visual language:** Chinese wuxia references, an ivory inner robe under a deep green split outer layer, fitted dark sleeves, a red sword tassel, and ink stains on the thumb. Her clothes allow quick turns. The tassel extends the gesture of a cut without becoming the weapon itself.

**Combat:** jian thrusts, redirection, and attacks that mark two enemies for a linked displacement. Her future mechanics turn the placement of opponents into a readable sentence.

**Signature encounter:** The Rain Pavilion, where a beloved provincial poet uses a bell to make his own account of a massacre the only one anyone remembers. Lin must preserve the witnesses while fighting the author of a beautiful lie.

## Meera — the keeper of the last crossing

Meera comes from a coastal community informed specifically by Kerala's landscape and martial traditions. Her family maintains a ferry and a training courtyard. She grows up learning how weight travels through a boat, through a body, and through a flexible blade. Her elder brother teaches her to stop showing off when someone else has to repair the damage.

During the monsoon, an Accord vessel runs aground carrying sealed bronze funeral vessels. Meera helps the crew before discovering that each container holds names taken from communities far inland. The captain orders the cargo burned. Her brother refuses. The ensuing struggle sinks the ferry and scatters the vessels along the river.

Meera survives with one vessel that will not open. It speaks in her brother's voice but knows things he could never have known. She follows the river upstream to discover whether it contains him, an imitation, or several people forced into the shape of someone she loves.

She is direct, playful, mechanically inventive, and quick to notice when solemn people are hiding behind solemnity. Her anger is not constant fury. It is an inability to tolerate institutions calling an avoidable loss inevitable.

**Visual language:** cream wrapped fabric with restrained gold edging, crimson waist cloth, dark practical protective pieces, and brass fasteners. Movement and grounded stance draw from researched Kerala martial references. The urumi-inspired weapon is a fantasy adaptation, to be reviewed with knowledgeable practitioners before final animation.

**Combat:** broad circular control, momentum management, and a narrow finishing lash. She must maintain safe distance to build power. Enemies entering the circle force a deliberate change of rhythm.

**Signature encounter:** The Monsoon Courtyard, occupied by a former teacher who has promised to return the dead if Meera stops opening the vessels. The fight asks whether a comforting voice is enough to make a person real.

## Where their stories meet

Ren needs testimony; Lin carries it. Lin needs the original legal chain; Eleanor possesses its seal. Eleanor needs to locate the vessels her warrant delivered; Meera has traced their route. Meera needs someone who can distinguish a preserved memory from an imposed one; Ren's sister was the first person to interrupt the process.

Their alliance is earned through disagreement. Eleanor wants a public trial. Ren thinks the bells must be destroyed. Lin fears destroying the only remaining records. Meera refuses any plan that treats the people inside as evidence rather than people.

The campaign's final choice must grow from these conflicts. Do not resolve them with a new villain who absolves everyone. The Accord was built by people who preferred a quiet world to an honest one; dismantling it means learning to live together after the truth returns.
